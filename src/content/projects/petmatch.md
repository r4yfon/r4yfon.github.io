Young people looking to buy or adopt a pet can usually only judge from the seller's photos. Medical history and temperament are rarely listed up front. PetMatch asks users about their lifestyle and living situation, then recommends the pets most likely to suit them. I was a UX designer in a six-person team, from research through to A/B testing, between August and November 2023.

[Figma prototype](https://www.figma.com/file/F67UC3AOVZW0nO0hox4XgV/PetMatch?type=design&node-id=16%3A5876&mode=design&t=BiivaI6iUMjb9sOu-1)

## Research

We ran [semi-structured interviews](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1s_GMJS2m2ZJLj5TRnAA4iXhW_QN4z0dd/edit?usp=drive_link&ouid=110999776598585166174&rtpof=true&sd=true) with 8 people aged 20 to 30, all studying or working full-time and all current or past pet owners. Three pain points came out of them:

- **No proper marketplace:** finding a pet meant trawling a long list of separate websites.
- **Expectations vs reality:** buyers can't verify what sellers claim, especially hidden issues like chronic conditions.
- **Not knowing the pet:** with only the seller's information to go on, it's hard to judge whether a pet is a good fit.

Our persona, **Max Tan, 22**, is a university student who lives in a four-room flat with his parents, grandparents and sister. He wants a dog that suits his lifestyle, needs his opinionated family on board, and has no time to visit pet shops and shelters in person.

## Borrowing from e-commerce and dating apps

Our goal was to close the gap between what users expect from a pet and what they get, so we studied apps that display and verify information about the things being chosen: e-commerce stores and dating apps. The lesson was to keep things simple and clear and, most importantly, to rank information by what users care about most. That ranking shaped the [sitemap](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1VcBEWIQZABMEcnzl-GP0L3hokHlWkNU3/view?usp=drive_link).

## Prototyping and testing

- **Paper first:** we iterated on paper prototypes, walking Max through an [easy and a hard scenario](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1Bhk_0Ub6ZHRkrHHpgENXCLapGqxpHDpm/edit?usp=sharing&ouid=108311626464836977838&rtpof=true&sd=true) before moving to digital.
- **Heuristic evaluation:** another team reviewed our design against [Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/), giving us fresh eyes on it.
- **A/B test:** I built the interactive hi-fi prototype in Figma. We then ran a within-subjects A/B test on Maze with 30 participants to decide where a key button should go, and analysed the results with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test ([analysis](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1Lg1P7LG_U0v6EQNqqexwT7oO_fmwWkiv/edit?usp=drive_link&ouid=110999776598585166174&rtpof=true&sd=true)).

## Takeaway

Some design choices I'd have ruled out without a second thought turned out to be what users preferred in testing. Since then, I don't treat any feature or layout as settled until users have seen it. Data beats intuition.
